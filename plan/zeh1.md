## The most promising construction

I would frame this as a bulk-to-boundary consistency problem: can a single gravitational solution satisfy the constraints governing the universe’s spatial structure and also predict the evolution of the surfaces along which we observe it?

Three ingredients are needed.

### 1. The spatial constraint must respond to the cosmic web

In general relativity, Buchert’s averaging framework gives, for irrotational pressureless matter,

\\[ 3H\_{\mathcal D}^{2} =8\pi G\langle\rho\rangle\_{\mathcal D} +\Lambda -\frac12\langle{}^{(3)}R\rangle\_{\mathcal D} -\frac12 Q\_{\mathcal D}. \\]

Here, \\(H\_{\mathcal D}\\) describes the average expansion of a spatial domain, \\(\langle{}^{(3)}R\rangle\_{\mathcal D}\\) its average spatial curvature, and \\(Q\_{\mathcal D}\\) the kinematical backreaction generated by variations in local expansion and shear.

The important point is that curvature and inhomogeneity enter the averaged expansion equations. They are not arbitrary additions: their evolution must also obey an integrability condition. This is an established mathematical framework, although its implications for observed cosmic acceleration remain debated.&#x20;

### 2. The same spacetime must determine the causal boundary

The next step is not to assume that the spatially averaged scale factor automatically describes a horizon. Instead, propagate light rays through the same inhomogeneous spacetime and calculate how their cross-sectional areas, distances and redshifts evolve.

There is already a concrete foundation for this. Buchert, van Elst and Heinesen developed a covariant averaging formalism on an observer’s past light cone. Its area-averaged constraint includes matter density, the cosmological constant, curvature and optical backreaction; the framework also treats observables such as angular-diameter distance and cosmological redshift drift. Crucially, it identifies additional variables and closure conditions that the equations alone do not fix.&#x20;

That is close to the structure we are looking for. But it does not yet prove that one unique relation connects spatial backreaction to the evolution of causal boundaries.

### 3. The connection must restrict observations, not merely describe them

This is where the research opportunity lies.

Suppose a proposed cosmological model claims that the cosmic web’s inhomogeneity substantially changes average expansion. Once its matter content, geometry and boundary conditions are fixed, the model should be required to predict multiple observables from the same solution: expansion rates, distance–redshift relations, redshift drift and the effects of gravitational lensing.

The test would then be:

- Infer the relevant inhomogeneity and curvature from observations and a specified relativistic model.
- Calculate the light-cone observables from that model, rather than inserting the averaged expansion into homogeneous-universe formulas without justification.
- Test whether the predicted quantities agree simultaneously with the data.

The new restriction would be a nontrivial consistency relation or allowed range linking measured cosmic-web structure to light-propagation observables. A model that fits the expansion history but fails this joint test would be excluded, even if its expansion curve alone looked convincing.

This is an extension of an active research direction. Recent observational tests already exploit consistency relations of FLRW cosmology, and the October 2026 work of Heinesen and Clifton proposes a new null test to isolate cosmologies with non-FLRW observational relations. The additional challenge would be to make such a test quantitatively specific to the cosmic web and its backreaction.&#x20;

## What about the future event horizon?

There is an important distinction. Our past light cone is directly connected to the observations we receive. A future event horizon, by contrast, depends on the universe’s entire future evolution. Its location cannot be inferred model-independently from present observations alone.

I would therefore start with light-cone observables and a carefully defined apparent or other quasi-local boundary. The future event horizon can become a further consequence of the model, but it should not be treated as a directly measured energy reservoir.

Likewise, the Hamiltonian constraint of general relativity is not by itself proof that all positive matter energy cancels all negative gravitational energy globally. A global-energy interpretation requires a specific definition and appropriate boundary conditions. The proposed connection must not quietly assume the zero-energy hypothesis it is meant to investigate.&#x20;

## The decisive missing step

The hard part is establishing a relation between two different kinds of averaging: averages over spatial slices and averages over null light fronts. They are not automatically interchangeable. A convincing result must derive their connection from a specified theory and show that the resulting observable relation is not adjustable away by introducing unconstrained functions.

Mathematically plausible

General relativity already provides constraint equations, spatial-averaging equations, and covariant machinery for following observables along light cones.

Not yet established in the proposed form

I have not identified a demonstrated theorem that unifies the global constraint, cosmic-web backreaction and causal-boundary evolution into the specific new restriction described here.

A concrete research target

Derive a closed, testable relation between spatial backreaction and null-cone observables, with fewer free functions than the observations it must explain.

The deeper shift in perspective is this: instead of asking whether the universe’s expansion must be balanced by a hidden contraction, ask whether one underlying spacetime geometry must satisfy a joint ledger of bulk constraints and boundary observations.

That question is precise enough to attack mathematically, close enough to established relativistic cosmology to avoid inventing physics prematurely, and restrictive enough that a successful derivation could distinguish competing models of the universe.
